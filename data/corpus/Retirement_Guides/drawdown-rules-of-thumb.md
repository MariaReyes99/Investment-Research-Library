# Drawdown rules of thumb for spending savings in retirement (as at September 2026)

## What these rules are for
Once paid work stops, the question changes from "how much can I save?" to "how much can I spend each year without running out?". Spending savings down over retirement is called drawdown or decumulation. The Retirement Income Interest Group (RIIG) of the New Zealand Society of Actuaries has published four simple rules of thumb, modelled for New Zealand conditions and updated over time. Sorted's retirement navigator, run by Te Ara Ahunga Ora Retirement Commission, is built on these rules and helps people combine them with NZ Super.

Each rule sets how much to take from retirement savings (for example KiwiSaver) each year. NZ Super and any other income are spent on top. No rule is right for everyone: each gives a different amount of income, for a different length of time, with different risks.

## The 6% rule
Each year, take 6% of the value of the savings when you retired. The amount stays the same each year and is not raised for inflation, so it buys a little less each year.
- Suits: people who want more to spend in the early years of retirement, when many are most active.
- Trade-off: income falls in real terms over time, and there is a risk the savings run out within a long lifetime.

## The inflated 4% rule
Take 4% of the value of the savings at retirement, then increase that amount each year by inflation.
- Suits: people concerned about living a long time, or who want to leave an inheritance.
- Trade-off: a lower starting income than the 6% rule, in exchange for income that keeps its buying power and a better chance of money left over.

## The fixed date rule
Each year, divide what is left by the number of years until a chosen date (for example age 85 or 90). Income changes from year to year with investment returns, and the savings are designed to run out on that date.
- Suits: people who want to know when their savings will run out and are comfortable relying on NZ Super after that.
- Trade-off: after the date, income is NZ Super (and any other income) only.

## The life expectancy rule
Each year, divide what is left by the average remaining life expectancy at that age. Because life expectancy is recalculated each year, the savings are not fully used up, and the rule provides some income for life.
- Suits: people who want to make the most of their savings across their whole retirement.
- Trade-off: income varies with returns and needs a fresh calculation every year using life expectancy tables. Sorted's retirement navigator does this calculation.

## Using the rules with this library's wealth projector
The projector offers the 6% rule, the inflated 4% rule and the fixed date rule as ways to spend savings in retirement, alongside spending a set amount and a flexible "guardrails" approach. It does not model the life expectancy rule, which needs official life expectancy tables; use Sorted's retirement navigator for that one.

## Points to remember
- These are rules of thumb, not advice. They assume savings stay invested, so returns, fees and the type of fund all affect how long money lasts.
- Growth-type funds give higher expected returns with bigger ups and downs; the rules still apply, but income will vary more under the fixed date and life expectancy rules.
- NZ Super is the base for most New Zealanders. The rules are about spending savings on top of it.
- For a personal recommendation, talk to a licensed financial adviser.

Sources: New Zealand Society of Actuaries, Retirement Income Interest Group, "Decumulation Options in the New Zealand Market: How Rules of Thumb can help" and later updates; Te Ara Ahunga Ora Retirement Commission, Sorted retirement navigator.
