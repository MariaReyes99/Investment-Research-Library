# Compounding and wealth projections

## How compounding works
Compounding means returns earn their own returns. If a portfolio earns 7% a year, the second year's 7% is calculated on the original amount plus the first year's gain. Over short periods the effect is small; over decades it dominates. At 7% a year, money roughly doubles every 10 years (the "rule of 72": 72 ÷ 7 ≈ 10.3 years).

Regular contributions compound too. Someone who adds a fixed amount each month builds wealth from two sources: new money, and growth on everything already invested. Early contributions matter most because they have the longest time to grow.

## Nominal versus real (today's dollars)
A projection in "future dollars" (nominal) looks larger than it feels. With 2.5% inflation, NZD 1,000,000 in 20 years buys about what NZD 610,000 buys today. Good projections show results in today's dollars so they can be compared with today's living costs. The library's calculator reports both, and uses today's dollars for goals.

## Fees and tax drag
A fee of 1% a year sounds small, but it is taken from the whole balance every year. Over 30 years, the difference between a 0.2% and a 1.2% fee on the same underlying return can reduce the final balance by roughly a quarter. Tax works the same way: in New Zealand, PIE funds and FIF income are taxed each year, which lowers the rate at which a portfolio compounds. The calculator subtracts fees and an optional tax drag from the expected return.

## Why projections use ranges
Markets do not deliver a steady average return. A projection with one number hides that uncertainty. The library shows:
- Low, Expected and High scenarios (the expected return minus and plus 2 percentage points), and
- a chance of reaching the goal, estimated by simulating 2,000 possible market paths with year-to-year volatility.

Sequence matters: a large fall just before or after retirement does more damage than the same fall early in a working life, because more money is exposed and less time remains to recover.

## Choosing an expected return
Long-run assumptions used by fund managers and regulators are usually lower than recent share-market returns. For planning, many people test a range, for example 4–5% a year before fees for a conservative mix, 5–6% for balanced, and 6–8% for growth-oriented portfolios, then check how sensitive their plan is to the lower end. These are illustrations for testing a plan, not forecasts.
