# Index funds and why costs matter

## The core idea
An index fund buys every company in an index (such as the S&P 500 or MSCI World) in proportion to its size, instead of trying to pick winners. John Bogle, founder of Vanguard, built the first index fund for individual investors in 1976 and argued a simple point: before costs, all investors together earn the market return, so after costs the average active investor must earn less than the market. Every dollar paid in fees is a dollar of return lost.

## The evidence on active management
S&P Dow Jones Indices publishes regular SPIVA scorecards comparing active funds with their benchmarks. Over 10–15 year periods, a large majority of actively managed share funds in most markets, including Australia, have underperformed their benchmark index after fees. Past winners also tend not to stay winners.

## Costs to compare
- Management fee or expense ratio: the yearly percentage charged by the fund. Broad index ETFs commonly charge well under 0.25% a year; many active funds charge 1% or more.
- Platform or brokerage fees: what the investment app or broker charges to buy, sell or hold.
- Currency conversion fees: charged when converting NZD to USD to buy offshore ETFs. These can exceed the fund fee for small, frequent purchases.
- Tax: turnover and fund structure affect tax. For NZ investors see the FIF tax guide.

Check the current fee on the issuer's factsheet before comparing, because fees change.

## What indexing does not solve
Index funds still fall with the market. Choosing which index (US only, global, including emerging markets, sector funds) is itself a decision with large effects on risk.
