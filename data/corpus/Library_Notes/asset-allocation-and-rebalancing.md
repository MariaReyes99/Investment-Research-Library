# Asset allocation, diversification and rebalancing

## Asset allocation
Asset allocation is the split of a portfolio between growth assets (shares, property) and income assets (bonds, cash). It explains most of the difference in risk between portfolios. More shares means higher expected long-run returns and larger falls along the way. Common labels (used by KiwiSaver funds too) run roughly: conservative (about 20% growth assets), balanced (about 50–60%), growth (about 80%) and aggressive (close to 100%). Exact splits vary by provider.

## Time horizon and capacity for loss
Money needed within a few years is usually held in lower-risk assets, because a share-market fall could not be waited out. Money not needed for decades can ride out falls. A traditional rule of thumb set the bond share near one's age; modern approaches vary widely, and many investors keep higher share allocations because they live longer.

## Diversification
Holding many companies across countries and sectors reduces the damage any single failure can do. A broad global index fund (for example one tracking the MSCI World or MSCI ACWI index) holds well over a thousand companies. Concentrated funds, such as a Nasdaq-100 fund or a semiconductor sector fund, hold far fewer and can fall much further in a downturn. Owning several funds that track overlapping indexes (for example an S&P 500 fund plus a world fund) adds less diversification than it appears, because the world index is dominated by the same large US companies.

## Rebalancing
Over time, the best-performing assets grow into a larger share of the portfolio, raising risk. Rebalancing means selling some of what has grown and buying what has lagged, back to the target mix. Common approaches are on a calendar (for example once a year) or when an asset drifts more than a set amount (for example 5 percentage points) from target. Directing new contributions to the underweight asset rebalances without selling, which avoids transaction costs.
