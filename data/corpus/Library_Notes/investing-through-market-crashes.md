# Investing psychology: market crashes, FOMO and staying the course

## Falls are normal
Large share-market falls happen regularly. The S&P 500 fell about 57% from its October 2007 peak to March 2009, and about 34% in roughly a month in early 2020 before recovering within months. Falls of 10% or more happen in most years at some point. Anyone holding shares for decades should expect several falls of 30% or more.

## The cost of selling in a fall
Selling after a fall turns a paper loss into a real one, and the harder question becomes when to buy back. Some of the market's best days historically came very close to its worst days, often during the same crisis. Investors who sell and wait for calm frequently miss part of the recovery. Studies of fund flows also find that the average investor earns less than the funds they invest in, because money tends to flow in after strong returns and out after falls.

## Common behavioural traps
- Loss aversion: losses feel roughly twice as painful as equal gains feel good, which pushes people to sell at the wrong time.
- FOMO and recency bias: assuming recent strong performance (for example in technology or semiconductor shares) will continue, and concentrating a portfolio in it.
- Overconfidence: trading often, which adds costs and tax.
- Checking too often: looking at a portfolio daily makes normal volatility feel like constant loss.

## Tools that help
- A written plan: target allocation, when to rebalance, and what you will do in a 30% fall, decided in advance.
- Holding enough cash or conservative assets for near-term needs so shares never have to be sold in a crash.
- Automating contributions so investing continues regardless of headlines.
- Checking whether the reason for owning an investment has changed, rather than its price.
